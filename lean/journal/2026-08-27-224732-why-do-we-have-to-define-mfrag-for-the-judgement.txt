❯ Why do we have to define MFrag for the judgement work?                        

⏺ Let me look at where MFrag is defined and how the judgment layer docs motivate
  it.

  Searched for 2 patterns, ran 2 shell commands (ctrl+o to expand)

⏺ Short answer: because Judge is total over the syntax but preservation is not, 
  and something syntactic has to hold that boundary — the role that infer's 
  partiality played in the old checker-based spine.

  The detail (from Frag.lean's header and judgment-layer.md):

  1. The invariant's eval arm is existential over derivations. The
     machine-typing invariant InvJ/step_okJ says "the expression being evaluated
     has some Judge derivation." When preservation inverts that at a given
     head, cases surfaces every Judge rule whose conclusion could match that
     head. Judge deliberately covers all 47 expression heads (so the judgment
     can state things about full programs), but only a subset has its
     preservation cases proved. Without a gate, admitting the invariant at any
     state would drag every rule's preservation obligation in at once — you'd
     owe the whole mountain before proving anything.
  2. MFrag is the gate the eval arm conjoins. The invariant becomes "judged and
     in MFrag." Now, when the machine steps into an out-of-fragment head,
     preservation refutes that case by one MFrag inversion (a syntactic check)
     instead of by typing work. In the old chk/infer spine this refutation came
     for free: infer was partial and simply returned nothing outside its
     fragment. An inductive Judge has no partiality, so the fragment boundary
     has to be reified as its own predicate. MFrag growing head-by-head is
     literally the J-ladder's progress marker — each new head admitted means its
     preservation cases got proved.
  3. Since J31 it also carries the semantic-axiom door. MFrag is indexed by the
     axiom set A: a claimed expression with an out-of-fragment head enters via
     the semantic arm (guarded by fragHead e = false, so the syntactic and
     semantic routes never compete at the same head, and only in seq statement
     position so the coupling premises preservation needs are available). That's
     how a program containing a semantic leaf like lambda { 1 } stays
     machine-typed end to end.

  So MFrag isn't a second type system — SemJudge stays MFrag-free and
  reachability-defined. It's purely a proof-engineering device: a decidable
  syntactic fence that keeps preservation's obligation set matched to what's
  actually been proved, while letting the fragment (and the axiom-claimed
  leaves) grow incrementally.

❯ Why is preservation not total over the syntax?                                

  Searched for 2 patterns, ran 1 shell command (ctrl+o to expand)

⏺ It's not one reason — the out-of-fragment heads fall into three genuinely
  different buckets, and only one of them is "we just haven't done the work
  yet."

  1. Unclimbed rungs (true but unproved). Heads like cpath, splat elements,
  super/zsuper, and the return/next jump channels are believed preservable, but
  each preservation case is real proof work: you have to show that when the
  machine steps into that construct, every intermediate configuration —
  including the continuation frames it pushes — stays typed under KontOkJ/InvJ.
  The jumps are an instructive sub-case: they're not even reachable obligations
  until some running context opens those channels (the W8 method signatures), so
  proving them now would be typing states the invariant can't yet produce. The
  fragment is the ledger of which of these bills have been paid.

  2. Rules where preservation would be false as written. The J8 begin/rescue
  rule is the clean example (implementation-notes.md:83): transcribed from chk,
  it judges handlers at the region's entry environment. But if the body retypes
  a local (x = "s" after x : Int) and then raises, the handler actually runs at
  the mid-body environment — the entry-env assumption about x is simply wrong
  there. So this isn't a missing proof; attempting the preservation case would
  refute the rule. Admitting that head means first fixing the rule (mask
  body-assigned locals, or per-raise-point environments). This is exactly why
  preservation-over-everything can't be assumed: some of Judge's 47-head
  coverage is transcription of chk rules that were never in chk's sound tier
  either.

  3. Missing type-level machinery. A send named call stays out even though sends
  are in, because Judge.sendCall eliminates an arrow type and no machine-typed
  value introduces one yet (the J16/J19 "arrow bill"). Preservation for
  elimination is unprovable until the value typing VTy has a witness for arrows
  — there's nothing for the receiver's typing to invert into. Blocks/lambda sit
  behind the same bill.

  And behind all three there's the structural fact: this is Ruby. Some
  constructs (the metaprogramming/Rails direction) have no sound syntactic
  typing rule at any price — that's precisely why SemJudge exists as the
  reachability-defined ground truth and why J31 added the semantic-axiom door.
  For those, "preservation" is discharged per-claim by executing the semantics
  (EvalOkAt obligations), not by a rule case. So totality of preservation over
  the syntax isn't just deferred — for a slice of the language it's not the plan
  at all; MFrag is the fence that keeps the proved-syntactic slice, the
  claimed-semantic leaves, and the not-yet-touched heads from contaminating each
  other's obligations.

⏺ For the Homebrew work, the non-totality of preservation is the whole cost
  model — and MFrag is sufficient as the mechanism, but not yet as populated.
  The distinction matters.

  As a mechanism, yes — nothing on the slice falls outside its two routes. The
  J37 census (--census-j) walks each file and reports exactly what MFrag
  refuses, and every blocker lands in one of two buckets: (1) a claimable leaf —
  statement position, fragHead = false, one-step-reducible, so the J31 semantic
  arm can admit it with a dischargeable EvalOkAt obligation (the
  define_method/lambda pilots), or (2) a machine-coverage rung — grow the
  fragment by one head and pay its preservation case. The census found no third
  category, so the architecture doesn't need another door. But it also delivered
  the sobering finding that shapes the plan: claims are leaves, not containers.
  Five of eight files wrap everything in module Homebrew, which is technically
  claimable — but a container's EvalOkAt obligation is never dischargeable, so
  you can't claim away the wrapper; the honest work underneath is all rungs. The
  semantic route removes the type-system obstacle, not the machine-coverage
  one.

  As populated, no — zero of eight files accept today, and the bill is itemized.
  Because certification is whole-program (Machine.init p), a single
  out-of-fragment head anywhere blocks the file. After the J38–J42 rungs (cpath,
  return, hash, toplevel casgn, the emitter), the first accept — stripped
  vulns/semver.rb — costs exactly three more rungs:

  - module' and defs, both behind Wall 2: a fresh module/class object is an
    allocating definition — not PlainGrow, and TypeAgree's ancestors clause is
    false across it — so the entry fee is a class-allocation grow relation with
    its own transport suite. This is precisely what the current branch is paying
    down (J43f's ClsInv, J44's Decls.modules/NoHook in the recent commits).
  - One real block-send, behind Wall 1 on the J-spine: the Static-side invariant
    work is already proved (Proof/Static/Iter.lean, L242–L255); the rung
    transliterates it to KontOkJ.iterK and admits Judge.sendIter0/A into
    preservation.

  Beyond semver, the tails are honest: kwargs mass (24 in identify.rb, ~56 each
  in version.rb/vulnerability.rb) and send-block (60+ each in the two big files)
  are all machine rungs. And two blockers aren't just unproved-rung-shaped:
  begin/rescue in vulnerability.rb sits behind the J8 soundness fix (the
  entry-env handler rule must be changed, not just proved), and parameterized
  user methods stay permanently carried rows under the current UserConformsJ
  (params = [] pin) — a recorded bill, not a rung.

  So the practical answer: MFrag plus the semantic arm is the right and complete
  vocabulary for the slice — the ongoing work is exactly the rung ladder that
  populates it, currently mid-Wall-2, with semver.rb's whole-file accept as the
  next ratchet click.

❯ What does "transport suite" mean?                                             

  Searched for 2 patterns, ran 1 shell command (ctrl+o to expand)

⏺ "Transport" here is the proof-engineering term this codebase uses for carrying
  an invariant clause across a state-changing step, and a "transport suite" is
  the family of such lemmas that a given kind of step requires — one per
  invariant component — all stated against a shared "grow relation" describing
  what that step does to the heap.

  Concretely, from Proof/HeapGrow.lean: when the machine allocates a plain
  (non-class) object, the proof doesn't reason about the raw before/after heaps
  at every preservation case. Instead there's one relation, PlainGrow h h',
  capturing exactly what such a step supplies — the heap only grows, every old
  id reads back identically, and classPayload? agrees at every id. Then the
  suite is the collection of lemmas saying each thing the invariant is made of
  survives that relation:

  - PlainGrow.className_eq, classOf_eq, ancestors_eq, lookup_go_grow — the
    resolution reads answer the same in the new heap;
  - chainsIn_plainGrow — the chain well-formedness clause re-establishes;
  - plus, on the judgment spine, the ValueTy/KontOkJ/EntryOkJ/DeclsOkJ versions
    (the "table transports, J-flavored" section of Proof/Judgment/Mono.lean).

  Every preservation case for a heap-touching rule is then structured as: show
  the step produces PlainGrow h h', then discharge each invariant clause by one
  application of the corresponding transport lemma. The suite is reusable — a
  new rule whose step is plain-growing pays nothing new.

  Why Wall 2 needs "its own transport suite": allocating a module/class object
  breaks PlainGrow's load-bearing clause — the fresh id has a .cls payload, so
  classPayload? no longer agrees globally, and TypeAgree's unrelativized
  ancestors clause is outright false across the step. None of the existing
  lemmas apply. So the entry fee is a new grow relation (a class-allocation
  grow, the ClsGrow/ClsInv work in the recent J43/J44 commits) plus re-proving
  the whole family against it — every invariant component needs its "survives a
  class allocation" lemma, including genuinely new obligations the plain case
  never had (the ancestors walk now can reach the fresh class, hence the
  NoHook/Decls.modules clauses). That re-proved family is the transport suite
  the module' and defs rungs are priced at.

❯ Why is proving transport lemmata proving to be difficult? Or more generally,  
  why are these walls so hard to overcome?                                      

  Ran 1 shell command (ctrl+o to expand)

⏺ The short version: a wall is where a step stops merely growing data under the
  type structure and starts mutating the type structure itself. In this model
  the heap is Ruby's class table — ancestors, lookup, className are heap walks —
  so allocating a module changes the ground the entire invariant stands on, and
  every lemma keyed on "the type-relevant heap is fixed" dies simultaneously.
  The J43/J44 notes show five distinct ways that cost lands:

  1. The old transport suite's power came from one global clause, and the wall 
  breaks exactly that clause. PlainGrow works because classPayload? agrees at
  every id — so no congruence needs to know whether a walk stays inside the old
  heap; agreement is global. A class allocation falsifies that at the fresh id
  and nothing else. The replacement (ClsGrow) can only offer relativized
  congruences — "an old id's walk never reaches the fresh id" — and that
  relativization isn't provable from the existing invariant. It needs a new
  well-formedness clause (ChainsIn: no in-bounds object has a chain edge
  pointing out of bounds) just to be stateable. So the entry fee includes
  discovering, deciding, boot-discharging, and threading a new InvJ conjunct
  through every construction site (~15 sites, the hstr.2.1.* renumbering) before
  the first congruence can be written.

  2. Preservation quantifies over arbitrary conformant heaps, so branches no 
  real program hits must still be typed. Pricing the module' eval case surfaced
  two: a conformant heap may already contain an undeclared constant named Vulns
  (so the reopen branch fires against an aliased object and breaks StackCtx's
  naming clause), and a hit on a non-module value raises where CtlOkJ has no
  raise vocabulary — preservation would be false outright, not just unproved.
  The fix isn't proof labor; it's a design change to the judgment: a new
  certificate-visible channel (Decls.modules, ModuleNameOk) whose invariant pins
  which branch runs. That's the deeper reason walls are slow — they keep
  converting "prove a lemma" into "redesign the invariant so the lemma becomes
  true."

  3. New channels couple every other rule. Once DeclsOkJ carries ModuleNameOk
  per declared name, every heap-writing rule owes its preservation — casgn needs
  a new freshness guard ("not a declared module name"), and each existing
  transport family grows one more clause. Invariant conjuncts multiply the
  suite: N clauses × M step shapes.

  4. Facts proved at the wrong granularity don't compose. J44's NoHook example:
  it was stated per-value (lookup h v hook = none), but the fresh module's
  dispatch chain is [eigen] ++ ancestors Boot.classId — a list no old value ever
  dispatched along, so no old fact applies. The honest fix is rephrasing the
  clause per-table (which implies the per-value form) and re-transporting it.
  You only discover these mismatches by attempting the eval case.

  5. One source step is a composite of heap operations. module' is ClsGrow ∘ 
  constSetIn ∘ ClsGrow ∘ EigenSet ∘ push — two allocations, a constant write, an
  eigen-set, a frame push — and the invariant must survive each intermediate
  heap, with the fresh-side facts (NoHook/Saturated/ClassOk/ChainsIn on the
  literal fresh chains) computed by hand.

  Plus the mundane Lean friction underneath (omega failing under large contexts,
  autoParam positioning), which taxes every one of the above.

  Why the walls specifically and not the other rungs: cpath, return, hash were
  cheap because they either read the heap or grow it plainly — the existing
  suite applies verbatim, so each rung was "one kont arm + one delivery case."
  Walls 1 and 2 are precisely the two places the machine does something the
  invariant's transport vocabulary has never described (block frames;
  class-table growth), so the first instance pays for the vocabulary itself. The
  consolation, visible in the ratchet: it's an entry fee, not a toll — J43d–f's
  congruence stack (ClsCongr/ClsValues/ClsInv) is reusable, and
  defs-in-a-fresh-module then costs one defineMethod whose transports already
  exist.

❯ Do we still have a heap monotonicity hypothesis that we're imposing?          

  Searched for 1 pattern, ran 2 shell commands (ctrl+o to expand)

⏺ Yes and no — "heap monotonicity" splits into three different things here, and
  only one of them is global, and it's proved, not assumed:

  1. Size/identity monotonicity: a proved global fact. Step.heap_monotone
  (Proof/Adequacy.lean:59) proves by induction over the step relation that the
  heap never shrinks and ObjIds are never reused. That's not a hypothesis we
  impose — it's a true fact of the machine, which has no GC or deallocation.
  (It's also honest about the model's scope: the Lean machine deliberately never
  frees, so id-stability is real, not idealized.)

  2. Content monotonicity: deliberately not assumed. There is no global "old ids
  read back identically" hypothesis. PlainGrow.get pins old ids, but PlainGrow
  is a per-step-shape fact established at each producer — and plenty of steps
  don't satisfy it. defineMethod, constSetIn, ivar writes, and the eigen-set all
  mutate existing objects, and each has its own relation and transport family
  (IvarOnly, the constSetIn suite, EigenSet, the defineMethod transports). The
  module' composite is exactly a chain of these different shapes. So the proofs
  are keyed to what each step actually does to the heap, not to an append-only
  assumption. Relatedly, Proof/Static/Konts.lean records that DeclsOk is
  "neither monotone nor antitone" — the architecture explicitly gave up on a
  single monotonicity story there.

  3. Table monotonicity: proved, but gated and carrying real hypotheses — this 
  is the one closest to "imposed." judge_mono (Proof/Judgment/Mono.lean, J23)
  proves Judge is monotone along SubDecls, but only over the MFrag-gated 
  fragment — Judge's full rule set is genuinely non-monotone
  (alias'/defDecl/defPromote carry freshness guards that a bigger table can
  falsify). And the gate has been tightening as channels grow: the J42 casgn
  design added a top = false hypothesis (a toplevel casgn grows wconsts,
  falsifying the D' = D conclusion), and J44's SubDecls pins Decls.modules equal
  rather than letting it grow. So where a monotonicity assumption survives,
  it's as explicit hypotheses on a proved lemma whose consumers are checked to
  sit inside the gate — every time a new channel would break it, the recorded
  move has been to weaken the lemma's scope rather than assume the property.

  The axiom-cleanliness checks (check-proofs.sh) are the backstop: nothing in
  this area is an unproven axiom or sorry — the only assumed things in the whole
  layer are the user-declared SemAxioms claims, and those are discharged
  per-claim by executing the semantics.

❯ Can you think of any ways we can extend the semantic judgement beyond judging 
  a single expression evaluation? What if we were able to judge sequences of    
  machine steps/steps up to the next kont?                                      

⏺ Let me look at the actual SemJudge and EvalOkAt definitions to ground this.

  Ran 2 shell commands (ctrl+o to expand)

⏺ Yes — and the "up to the next kont" idea is, I think, exactly the right axis,
  because it attacks the two recorded limitations of the current design at once.
  Let me lay out what the current shape can't do and then the concrete
  extensions.

  What's limiting today. SemJudge is a whole-run judgment: it quantifies over
  conformant states with an empty continuation (m.kont = [] is baked into
  Conformant) and speaks only about final outcomes. Two consequences, both
  already recorded as bills in Sem.lean's header and the census findings:

  - No post-state: Γ' and D' aren't in SemJudge, so two SemJudge facts don't
    compose sequentially — you can't chain "run e₁, then e₂" semantically.
  - Claims are leaves in statement position only: the EvalOkAt obligation must
    re-establish InvJ in one evalExpr step at .any, delivered into the ambient
    kont. That's why module Homebrew wrappers can't be claimed and why a claim
    can't sit in an if condition or a send argument — a value-position claim
    would have to say what value arrives at the surrounding frame, which a
    whole-run judgment can't express.

  Extension 1 — the segment judgment (your "up to the next kont"), as a safety 
  property. Define something like:

  SemSeg A D Γ e c τ Γ' D' :=
    ∀ m K, ConformantAt A D c Γ m K → m.ctl = .eval e → m.kont = K →
      every state reachable before the machine pops below K is not type-stuck, ∧
      if the machine ever delivers to K, the delivered value inhabits τ
      and the state is conformant at (Γ', D')

  The key design choice: phrase delivery as a conditional ("if it ever pops back
  to K"), not a promise. That keeps it a safety property — provable by the same
  invariant reasoning, no step-indexing — which matters because §1.5 explicitly
  declined to buy step-indexing, and a total-correctness version ("it will
  deliver") would smuggle termination in. Notice this judgment is precisely the
  semantic counterpart of what CtlOkJ's eval arm + KontOkJ already say
  syntactically, rule by rule: you'd be reifying the preservation invariant's
  mid-machine shape as a first-class semantic statement, the same move J30 made
  for whole runs. The fundamental lemma (judge_semSeg) is again the already-paid
  step_okJ, repackaged.

  What it buys immediately: multi-step EvalOkAt. The current obligation is "one
  step re-establishes InvJ" — that's what makes containers unclaimable. A SemSeg
  obligation is "any number of steps, safety throughout, conformance at
  delivery." Then module Homebrew ... end is claimable: its segment is long but
  its delivery fact is simple. That would collapse a real fraction of the
  Homebrew rung ladder — the walls exist because machine-coverage rungs are the
  only way to get through a container; a segment claim is a second way, priced
  at one semantic lemma (or one bounded execution) instead of a transport suite.
  And value-position claims (send arguments, if conditions) stop needing a
  per-position coupling rung: the segment judgment carries the delivered τ/Γ'/D'
  the surrounding frame needs.

  Extension 2 — biorthogonal quantification over continuations. Instead of
  "empty kont" or "fixed K," define the judgment by interaction: e is
  semantically typed at τ iff for every KontOkJ-conformant continuation
  expecting τ, the composite machine is safe. This is the classic ⊤⊤-closure/CPS
  semantic typing. Its payoff is that composition becomes plugging —
  sequencing, argument positions, everything, uniformly — and it's the natural
  first-order shadow of what the H-layer already did: J36's route 3 (Iris WP per
  conformant state) is a judgment over machine-step sequences; a biorthogonal
  SemSeg would be its cheap sibling, with an adequacy lemma connecting them
  instead of two parallel stories.

  Extension 3 — the bounded/fuel-indexed variant, for discharge by execution.
  SemSeg_n: safety for n steps from every conformant state. This is
  decidable-ish per concrete start state (the concolic engine's territory) and
  gives the three-valued verdict structure bounded-effect-checking.md wants:
  proved segment / refuted (witness trace) / frontier at fuel n. A claim
  discharged at bound n composes into a certificate that is honest about its
  frontier — the check-vs-prove spectrum landing inside the judgment layer
  itself.

  Extension 4 — decorate the segment with its trace. Once the judgment is about
  a step sequence rather than an endpoint, it can speak about the sequence's
  labels: "this segment emits only effects in policy P," "this segment's heap
  delta touches only these class tables." The second one is worth flagging
  specifically: a semantic-diff/confinement claim over a module body's segment
  is exactly the "metaprogramming backdoor is a heap delta" story from the
  shared framing docs — the segment judgment is where that vocabulary would
  attach to the proved spine.

  The honest costs. (1) Conformant currently is "empty kont + closed jump
  channels"; a segment version needs a mid-machine conformance (ConformantAt … 
  K) — essentially exposing KontOkJ in the trusted statement, which grows the
  audit surface of what a certificate consumer must believe. (2) The
  perverse-pairing hazard J31 closed with fragHead-guarded .sub gets a new
  instance per admissible position — each position where a segment claim can sit
  needs its coupling premise, same as the recorded per-position rung note in
  Frag.lean. (3) Jump channels: a segment inside a method body has ret/inLoop
  open, so SemSeg needs the jump vocabulary (RetOkJ etc.) in its delivery clause
  — which conveniently is the same vocabulary the return rung already opened
  (J39).

  If I had to pick an order: Extension 1 phrased as safety, with the multi-step
  EvalOkAt as its first consumer and a claimed module body as the pilot — it's
  the smallest statement that dissolves the "claims are leaves" finding, and
  it's on the critical path of the Homebrew ratchet, unlike 2–4 which are
  architecture for later.

❯ What do you think is the extension that most lends itself to rapidly          
  expanding the covered fragment via targeted lemmata that judge uncovered      
  areas of the code (ideally generally, but if we have to, specific to the      
  homebrew slice), and why?                                                     

⏺ The segment judgment — but only if it's paired with a second extension that I
  under-weighted last turn: parameterized semantic axioms (claim schemas rather
  than claim leaves). Neither alone gives you rapid expansion; together they do.
  Let me argue it from the census numbers.

  Why per-site claims don't scale, even with segments. Suppose the segment
  judgment lands and containers become claimable. The current SemAxioms design
  matches claims by exact expression (cl.e = e). The Homebrew mass is send-block
  67 + kwargs 56 in version.rb alone, similar in vulnerability.rb. Sixty-seven
  hand-discharged EvalOkAt-style obligations is more work than the Wall-1 rung
  it would replace — the syntactic rung costs a transport suite once and then
  covers every instance free. Exact-match claims only beat rungs when instances
  are few (lambda { 1 } was fine as a leaf). So the rapid-expansion question is
  really: what makes one lemma cover many sites?

  The answer is schemas: semantic lemmas quantified over subterms. The blocked
  mass isn't 67 distinct constructs — it's a handful of patterns instantiated 67
  times: iterator sends (each/map/select over a machine-typed body), defs with
  a machine-typed body (install-and-forget, per the J43 note), module bodies
  whose statements are all covered, kwargs call sites against a claimed row. A
  schematic semantic lemma has the shape:

  ▎ for all b with MFrag A b and SemSeg … b … τb, the segment for xs.each { |x| 
  ▎ b } is safe and delivers .any

  One proof, dozens of sites. And here's why this is cheap rather than
  wall-priced: the proof content for the iterator schema already exists — the
  Static-side block-frame invariant work is proved (Proof/Static/Iter.lean,
  L242–L255). The Wall-1 rung is expensive because admitting Judge.sendIter0/A
  into preservation means building the full KontOkJ.iterK vocabulary so
  arbitrary judged code can flow through block frames forever after. A schema
  lemma only has to walk the segment for its own pattern, using the existing
  transports, with the body handled by its SemSeg premise — it buys coverage
  without buying vocabulary. That's the structural reason this route is faster:
  rungs pay per-head × per-invariant-clause; schemas pay per-pattern.

  Why the segment judgment is the prerequisite, not optional. A schema's premise
  ("the body is judged") and conclusion ("the whole send is safe and delivers 
  into the surrounding frame") are both mid-machine, mid-continuation
  statements. Whole-run SemJudge can't express either — you need the "safe up to
  delivery at K, conformant at (τ, Γ′, D′)" form to state the lemma at all, and
  you need multi-step obligations because an iterator segment is many steps.
  So: segment judgment is the statement language, schemas are the multiplier.

  What it covers on the slice, concretely. Ranked by census mass: an iterator
  schema kills most of send-block (60+ each in the two big files, 1–6 elsewhere
  — including semver.rb's last blocker); a defs-with-typed-body schema kills
  defs (4–7 per file, in every file); a module-body schema (statements all
  covered ⇒ container covered) removes the wrapper problem generally instead of
  per-wall — and note it would have subsumed much of what Wall 2 is currently
  buying rung-by-rung. kwargs is the one mass I'd not route this way: kwargs
  sends sit in value position against specific rows, so the schema
  side-conditions degenerate to per-row facts — that one is honestly a machine
  rung (hash-packing kont), and it's transport-cheap since J40's hash work.
  T::Struct's prop rows (vulnerability.rb, not strippable) are the poster child
  for a slice-specific schema: "a prop statement installs a claimed row" —
  exactly the shape the doc already calls "certificate territory."

  The costs, stated honestly. (1) The checker changes character: exact-match
  claims are checked by one decide; schemas need instantiation-matching plus
  side-condition checks (MFrag of the bound subterms) in validateJ — the
  perverse-pairing hazard returns and must be re-closed per schema, the same
  discipline as fragHead-guarded .sub. (2) The trusted statement grows:
  SemAxiomsOk for a schema quantifies over instantiations, so a wrong side
  condition is a soundness hole rather than a dead certificate — schemas belong
  beside entryOkJ_even as Lean lemmas, not as user-supplied JSON. (3) It's
  deliberately a coverage shortcut, not vocabulary: code inside a schema-covered
  segment can't itself be judged syntactically deeper than the schema's
  premises reach. That's the right trade for the slice — and it's reversible,

⏺ You're right, and it's worth pinning down exactly where the drift can happen,
  because it's three distinct timepoints — and the failure shape is one the
  project has already met twice (J8's handlers-at-entry-env, and the redo note

  Drift point 1: the receiver's own evaluation. If the schema admits arbitrary
  receiver expressions, xs is evaluated inside the segment, and its evaluation
  can itself mutate the heap and table before the iteration starts. The body's
  premise would then need to hold at the receiver's out-state, not the claim's
  entry state — that's the Γ′/D′ threading the segment judgment carries, but it
  means the schema's premises are chained, not independent. The v1 discipline is
  the same one MFrag already uses for containers: restrict schema receivers to
  fragHead-true pure reads (a bare local, a literal), so the receiver's meaning
  is sampled in one step under entry conformance and the chaining collapses.

  Drift point 2: per-iteration environment drift — the J8 shape. The body's
  premise is stated at some environment Γ, but iteration n+1 starts at iteration
  n's out-environment. If the body can retype a local (x = "s" after x : Int),
  the premise you invoked at entry is false at the second iteration — exactly
  J8's counterexample transposed from handlers to loop bodies. So the schema
  can't just take SemSeg … b … Γ → …; it needs the body env-stable: its
  out-environment a SubEnv of its in-environment, making the entry env a genuine
  loop invariant. (The alternatives are J8's candidate fixes recurring: mask
  body-assigned locals out of the premise env, or quantify the body premise over
  every reachable iteration env — the latter is technically the honest
  statement but pushes the induction onto whoever discharges the premise.)
  Env-stability is checkable — it's a conclusion of the body's own judgment — so
  it's a legitimate schema side condition rather than trusted fine print.

  Drift point 3: the object vs. the name. Ruby's each samples the receiver once
  at dispatch — rebinding the local mid-iteration doesn't change what's
  iterated, but mutating the object does (xs.push inside the block). So the
  schema lemma's inductive invariant must be stated about the ObjId captured at
  send time, never about the name xs — the name's meaning is only consulted at
  drift point 1. Then heap mutation is the remaining threat: does the segment
  invariant survive the body pushing an ill-typed element into the very array
  being walked? Here the .any pilot earns its keep: if the schema delivers the
  block parameter at .any, element-type stability is vacuous and mutation during
  iteration is harmless — conformance (which the body's premise re-establishes)

❯ Do we need to change SemAxiomsOk to be in terms of SegOkAt? Why or why not?   

⏺ No — not now, and when the time comes it's an "add alongside," not a "change."
  Three reasons:

  1. Today it would break the mountain without buying anything. The only
  consumer of SemAxiomsOk is step_okJ's semantic-claim case, and that case must
  produce StepOkJ — whose .next arm demands InvJ at the successor state. An
  EvalOkAt obligation hands it exactly that; a SegOkAt obligation hands it
  StepOkS, whose .next arm may be RunSafe — and there is no way back from
  RunSafe m' to InvJ m' (that's the whole point of the absorbing arm). So
  redefining SemAxiomsOk over SegOkAt makes step_okJ unprovable as stated, and
  nothing exists yet (step_okS is J46) to consume the weaker form. Meanwhile no
  claim we can currently discharge needs the weaker form — every claim that can
  ride Judge.semantic today is one-step anyway.

  2. The implication only runs one way, so "change" means "strengthen every 
  composed theorem." SemAxiomsOk → SemAxiomsOkS (semAxiomsOk_toS); the converse
  is false. Since these predicates sit in hypothesis position of
  validateJ_certifies/judge_sound, swapping in the weaker one strengthens those
  theorems — which is precisely the content of J46's step_okS, not a
  definitional edit. Put differently: the definition change is the preservation
  re-proof; doing the former without the latter is just breakage.

  3. Even at J46, SegOkAt as currently built may not be the final shape. The J45
  note records the circularity hazard: for genuinely multi-step claims, the
  absorbing RunSafe conclusion covers the run past its own delivery, so
  discharging it needs S-grade soundness of the full axiom set — each obligation
  assuming all obligations. The likely resolution is a third,
  delivery-conditional shape (safe up to delivery, delivery re-establishes InvJ
  at the entry KontOkJ). Churning the definition of record onto the degenerate
  form now, then again onto the conditional form, is two migrations for one.

  There's also an audit-surface argument for never replacing it: SemAxiomsOk is
  the named condition a certificate consumer reads in validateJ_certifies's
  statement ("the composed theorem is conditional on exactly this"). Silently
  changing what that name means is exactly the kind of trusted-statement drift
  the layer has been careful to avoid. The right end state, post-J46:
  SemAxiomsOkS (or its delivery-conditional successor) appears explicitly in the
  new S-grade composed theorem; SemAxiomsOk survives as the stronger form that
  one-step dischargers naturally prove, embedded by semAxiomsOk_toS wherever the
  weak form is wanted. Existing certificates and lemmas (semAxiomsOk_lam,
  semAxiomsOk_lambdas) then work under both theorems unchanged.

  So the current state — both predicates defined, the embedding proved,
  SemAxiomsOk untouched as the definition of record — is the right resting point
  until step_okS exists.

❯ So how does InvS relate to InvJ?                                              

⏺ InvS is InvJ ∨ RunSafe, and the relationship has three layers worth
  separating: logical, dynamic, and proof-structural.

  Logically: strictly weaker as a predicate, equal in consequence. InvJ → InvS
  trivially (left injection), and the converse fails — a mid-segment state
  satisfies RunSafe but not InvJ (its control is an out-of-fragment expression
  no CtlOkJ arm describes). But once the axiom set is discharged, the collapse
  invJ_runSafe gives InvJ → RunSafe, so InvS ↔ RunSafe: the disjunction adds
  nothing semantically over its right arm. Both certify exactly the reachability
  property (¬ typeStuck plus result typing) — with one honest downgrade: InvJ
  alone also carries progress (an InvJ state never steps to
  .stuck/.unsupported), and that is lost in InvS, deliberately, because RunSafe
  — like SemJudge — never promised it.

  Dynamically: a one-way valve. In a run, InvJ states step to InvJ states
  (that's step_okJ) until the machine enters a semantically-claimed segment;
  there (once J46 wires it) the run crosses into the RunSafe arm and never comes
  back — RunSafe.step makes the right arm absorbing, so there is no re-entry
  obligation into the syntactic discipline. Note the crossing is at the
  invariant level, not the program level: the machine may well pop out of the
  segment back into syntactically judged code, but the proof doesn't need to
  notice, because RunSafe at the segment's entry already covered that entire
  future (which is also exactly why the multi-step obligation is hard to
  discharge — the circularity bill in the J45 note).

  Proof-structurally: the two arms have opposite cost profiles, and the 
  disjunction exists to pair them. InvJ is a conjunction of per-state,
  locally-checkable facts (heap well-formedness, frame conformance, KontOkJ, a
  Judge derivation for the control) — expensive to preserve (the whole
  transport-suite economy) but the only form that supports case-by-case step
  analysis and boot-time initiation by decide. RunSafe is a property of the
  entire future — trivially preserved (one line), but with no internal structure
  to induct on; it can only be established, either via the collapse from InvJ
  or by a direct semantic proof (a schema lemma, execution of the semantics). So
  InvS is the standard shape for grafting semantic facts into an inductive
  proof: the left arm is the establishment engine, the right arm is the
  absorption sink, and stepOkJ_toS/stepOkS_of_runSafe are the two case-closers

❯ Why do we need to absorb RunSafe in the first place in a segment lemma?       

⏺ Because the safety theorem is quantified over every reachable state, and a
  multi-step segment's interior states are reachable — so the invariant must
  hold something at them, and InvJ is false there by construction (the control
  is an out-of-fragment expression, the konts are shapes KontOkJ has no
  vocabulary for — that missing vocabulary being exactly the machine rung the
  schema route declined to build). The chain of forced moves:

  1. You can't skip the interior. invariant_sound concludes ¬ typeStuck at each
  reachable state by holding the invariant at that state and applying progress
  there. A type-stuck outcome can occur three steps into the segment, so the
  interior states can't be glossed as one big meta-step — each one needs an
  invariant arm that covers it.

  2. Whatever covers them must be step-closed, and a non-syntactic predicate can
  only close on itself by being future-facing. InvJ closes under steps because
  its per-state structure (judged control + typed konts) is transported case by
  case — that's the expensive machinery. A segment arm has no such structure to
  transport; the only way a structureless predicate survives an arbitrary step
  is if it already quantified over what comes next. So the segment arm is
  necessarily some form of "everything from here is fine" — a safety statement
  about the future.

  3. The only real choice is where the future ends, and absorption is the 
  degenerate choice. Two candidates:

  - RunSafe (absorbing): the future never ends — "the entire rest of the run is
    safe." Closure is one line (RunSafe.step: the successor's reachable set is a
    subset). No barrier bookkeeping, no re-entry obligation, definable with
    zero new vocabulary. The cost surfaces in the obligation: a discharger must
    certify the run past its own delivery, which drags in the soundness of the
    whole axiom set — the circularity bill.
  - SegInv (delivery-conditional): the future ends at the entry kont — "safe
    until the machine pops below K, and delivery re-establishes InvJ."
    Obligations become segment-local (no circularity), but now the predicate
    must carry the barrier as data (which kont suffix is K, what KontOkJ fact
    was true of it at entry), its closure lemma must show a mid-segment step
    stays above the barrier or delivers across it, and the delivery case owes an
    InvJ reconstruction. That's real new machinery — it's J46's actual bill.

  So "why absorb": absorption is what you get for free, and free was the right
  price for J45. It made the layer definable and provable in an afternoon with
  no new vocabulary, it is exactly sufficient for the claims that exist today
  (one-step claims, where the "future" the discharger certifies is a single
  delivery the existing EvalOkAt proof already handles — evalOkAt_segOkAt is the
  embedding), and it correctly stakes out the semantic grade of the whole
  layer. The delivery-conditional form isn't an alternative to absorption so
  much as absorption with a barrier installed — RunSafe is SegInv with K = ⊥ and
  re-entry never.

  One more distinction worth keeping sharp: absorption appears in two places,
  and only one is problematic. At the top of the composed theorem, everything
  collapses into RunSafe anyway (invS_runSafe) — harmless and unavoidable; the
  final claim is a whole-future statement. It's absorption inside the per-claim 
  obligation that costs: that's where "certify your own segment" silently became
  "certify everything after it too." J46's job is precisely to move the
  absorbing arm out of the obligation and keep it only in the final assembly.
